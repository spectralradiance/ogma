# First Light

Before the sky is dark it turns a colour that has no good name — deeper than blue, not yet black, the colour of ink held up to a lamp. That is when the first star appears, and more often than not it is not a star at all. It is Venus, bright and steady, a planet borrowing the Sun's light while the Sun itself has gone.

Stand outside long enough and the stars seem to arrive one by one. They do not arrive, of course; they were there all along. What changes is the eye. It takes the better part of half an hour for our night vision to wake, for the rods at the edge of sight to take over from the cones that serve us in daylight.

Night, then, is not something that falls. It is something we grow into, a little at a time, if we are patient enough to wait for it.

# Constellations

We cannot look at scattered points of light without joining them. It is the same instinct that finds faces in clouds, and it has filled the sky with hunters, bears and ladles for as long as anyone has been looking up.

The pictures are ours, not the sky's. The stars of a single constellation are rarely neighbours; one may be ten times farther away than the next, and they line up only from where we happen to stand. Change the vantage point and the figure falls apart.

That is perhaps why the same stars carry so many names. One culture sees a plough, another a dipper, another something else again. Astronomers today divide the sky into eighty-eight official regions, but that is a map, not a story. The stories were always a kind of language — the oldest one we have for talking about what is far away.

# Starlight and Time

Light is fast, but it is not instant. Sunlight is already eight minutes old by the time it reaches us, and the light of the stars is older still — years old, centuries old, sometimes far more.

So every star is seen as it was, not as it is. On a dark night the Andromeda Galaxy is just visible as a faint smudge, and the light that makes it left there two and a half million years ago, long before there was anyone here to see it.

To look up, then, is to look back. The night sky is the oldest thing most of us will ever see.
